Coronary heart disease develops when fatty plaque builds up in the arteries that supply blood to the heart. A heart attack happens when an artery becomes blocked [[7]](#source-7). It is a leading cause of death for Australian women. In 2025 it was the second leading cause after dementia, with 5,965 women dying from it [[1]](#source-1).

The gap is not only in who gets heart disease, but in how women are treated when they do. In a study of nearly 2,900 people treated for a major heart attack at 41 Australian hospitals between 2009 and 2016, women were about half as likely as men to have a coronary angiogram, and less likely to be given key medicines when they left hospital or to be referred to cardiac rehabilitation. They were about twice as likely to die within six months. These differences remained after accounting for how high-risk each patient was when they arrived, using a standard score that includes age [[2]](#source-2).

Symptoms are part of the story. Chest pain is the most common heart attack symptom in both women and men, but women under 50 more often have symptoms other than chest pain, such as pain in the jaw, shoulder or back, nausea, or breathlessness. The Heart Foundation's guide to [women's risk factors and warning signs](https://www.heartfoundation.org.au/your-heart/risk-factors-for-women) explains them [[6]](#source-6).

## Why it matters in Australia

The best-known Australian evidence on unequal heart attack care is now dated: it covers patients treated up to 2016. Newer data is more mixed. A 2024 study of 2,747 people treated with emergency stenting in Queensland between 2017 and 2020 found that women still waited longer for treatment and were less likely to be treated within the 90-minute target (50% compared with 58%). After accounting for other factors, being a woman was not linked to a higher risk of dying within 30 days, or of dying from heart disease within a year [[5]](#source-5). Faster, more consistent care may be narrowing the gap, but delays remain, and there is no recent national picture.

Many women have never had their heart health checked. In a 2023 survey, more than half said they had never seen their doctor for a general heart health check [[4]](#source-4). A Medicare-funded [Heart Health Check](/womens-health-gaps/where-were-headed/) with a GP is available to people aged 45 and over, or 30 and over for First Nations people.

## Global context

Women remain under-represented in the research that shapes heart care. Across 1,079 cardiovascular trials registered between 2017 and 2023, women made up 41% of participants, and they were least well represented, relative to how common each condition is in women, in trials for coronary heart disease, stroke and acute coronary syndrome [[3]](#source-3).
